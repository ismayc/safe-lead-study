How big a lead is safe, and is it still safe?

Every broadcast eventually asks whether a lead is safe. This study answers that question with the score at fixed moments of every regular-season game since the late 1990s: 35,492 NBA games from 1996-97 to 2025-26 and 6,469 WNBA games from 1997 to 2026. It asks how often the trailing team still wins, by lead and time left, and whether that has changed across six five-season eras. The short answer: in the NBA, a lead of 10 to 14 entering the fourth quarter is lost more often than it was, 10.1% of the time in the last five seasons against 5.8% in the first five, and the score margin swings more than it did. In the WNBA the samples are too small to say either way.

The question

Two ways to measure a safe lead

The study defines a lead as safe when the trailing team comes back to win less than 1% of the time. It reads the score every 5 seconds over the last two minutes of regulation and every 30 seconds before that, and it answers in two ways, each with its own claim.

The first is a count. For each lead, time left, and era, it takes the share of games the trailing team went on to win, overtime included. Those rates are smoothed so that a bigger lead is never less safe than a smaller one, and each comes with a 95% Wilson interval. To compare eras, the study uses six fixed game situations where the data are dense, such as a 10 to 14 point lead at the start of the fourth quarter, and puts a Newcombe interval on each change, with a Bonferroni adjustment for running 12 comparisons at once.

The second is a model. Hal Stern's 1994 Brownian-motion model treats the score margin as a random walk with one volatility, sigma, per era or season. It turns any lead and time left into a win chance, gives a smooth safe-lead curve, and makes the change in volatility one number with an interval.

Games enter only if their play-by-play final matches the league's game log and the running score never jumps by more than one basket on a single row; 55 NBA and 27 WNBA games fail those checks and are left out. Every table was computed twice, in Python and in R, and the two agree on every cell.

Then and now

A double-digit lead entering the fourth is less safe than it was

NBA teams that trailed by 10 to 14 entering the fourth quarter won 5.8% of the time from 1996-97 to 2000-01 (62 of 1,064 games) and 10.1% from 2021-22 to 2025-26 (118 of 1,167). The change is 4.3 percentage points, with a 95% interval of 2.0 to 6.5, and it stays above zero, 1.0 to 7.6, after the adjustment for multiple comparisons.

The rise was not gradual. The rate held between 7.6% and 7.8% for the three middle eras, then stepped from 7.8% to 10.2% into 2016-17 to 2020-21 and stayed there. Across the six NBA situations, three show a rise whose interval excludes zero (the start of the fourth quarter up 10 to 14, 6:00 left up 10 to 14, and 1:00 left up 5 or 6). After the adjustment, only the fourth-quarter one remains. The study does not test why the step happened.

The WNBA shows no clear change. None of its six situations has an interval that excludes zero: entering the fourth up 10 to 14, the trailing team won 3.5% of the time in 1997 to 2001 and 3.7% in 2022 to 2026, a change of +0.2 points with an interval of -4.1 to +3.9. But the WNBA samples in these situations are 18% the size of the NBA's, so a change as large as the NBA's cannot be ruled out either.

Safe leads

About 20 points is safe entering an NBA fourth quarter

Counting comebacks directly, the 1% safe lead at the start of the fourth quarter was 21 points in 1996-97 to 2000-01 (a range of 17 to 21 from the Wilson bounds) and 20 points in 2021-22 to 2025-26 (18 to 23). With 6:00 left it was 15 (13 to 17) and then 17 (13 to 19).

These single cells are noisy. The 1% tail rests on a few hundred games per cell, which is why a lead can read safer at 6:00 than at 3:00 in some eras, and why the era comparison above uses the denser middle of the distribution instead of the safe lead itself.

The random-walk model

The margin swings more than it used to

The model gives a cleaner view of the same change. NBA sigma rose from 2.43 (95% interval 2.34 to 2.53) in 1996-97 to 2000-01 to 2.78 (2.68 to 2.88) in 2021-22 to 2025-26, and the two intervals do not overlap. Most of that rise came at the same point as the comeback step: sigma was 2.55 in 2011-12 to 2015-16 and 2.81 in 2016-17 to 2020-21. The model's 1% safe lead entering the fourth quarter moved from 19.6 points (18.8 to 20.4) to 22.4 (21.6 to 23.2). In the WNBA, sigma went from 2.14 (1.96 to 2.34) to 2.44 (2.27 to 2.64), and those intervals overlap.

In the model, sigma links the two findings. With no drift, the model spreads the margin over the remaining minutes in proportion to sigma, so a wider spread leaves more of the outcomes below zero at the buzzer. From a 12-point lead entering the fourth quarter, the model gives the trailing team a 7.7% chance with the first era's sigma and 10.6% with the last era's. The observed rates for leads of 10 to 14 moved from 5.8% to 10.1%, in the same direction.

Where the model breaks

A random walk has no term for fouling or team strength

The model is a smooth summary, not a calculator for the endgame. In the NBA's last minute, leads of 7 to 10 are lost 0.17% of the time, against the model's 0.03%, or 6.2 times as often. The likely reasons are late-game fouling, three-point attempts, and timeouts, which add variance that a constant-rate random walk has no room for. The error runs the other way for big leads with time left: with 6 to 12 minutes to play, leads of 16 or more are lost 0.54% of the time against 0.79% predicted. Big leads belong disproportionately to better teams, and the model has no term for team strength (an interpretation, not tested here). For the endgame, the observed counts are the better guide.

Limits

What this does not show

These are regular-season games only; the playoffs are a different population. "Safe" is unconditional: a strong team's 10-point lead is safer than these tables say, and a weak team's is less safe. The running score comes from the league's play-by-play score stamps, and a stamp wrong by exactly two or three points would pass both data checks. The eras are fixed five-season blocks chosen before any result was seen, not fitted break points, and the six comparison situations were picked after a first look at comeback rates by era, so they are reported with both plain and adjusted intervals, whatever they show. Finally, the study measures that the NBA's fourth-quarter leads got less safe; it does not test why.
